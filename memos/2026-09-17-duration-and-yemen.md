# Duration: if Yemen goes, this is a 2027–2028 trade

**Date:** 17 September 2026
**Companion to:** [main thesis](2026-09-17-diesel-shortage.md) · [export-ban scenario](2026-09-17-export-ban-scenario.md) · [XOM/OXY review](2026-09-17-xom-oxy-review.md) · [tape log](tape-log.md)

**Verdict on the view: the evidence supports it, and my memos were too short-dated.** Extending the horizon from "harvest through Q1 2027" to a 2027–2028 structural window. One premise of the OXY sell is materially weaker and I am downgrading that call. The tanker leg gets much better, but it now has an expiry date I did not have before.

This is research, not investment advice.

---

## 1. Yemen: the recognized government is collapsing, not the Houthis

I framed the Houthis as a disruption input. Over the last week they became the dominant military actor on the Red Sea, and the direction is the opposite of what a "resolution" needs.

- **10 September:** Houthi forces took the port city of **Mokha** and the Hanish Islands.
- **11 September:** they took **Perim (Mayyun) Island at the centre of Bab el-Mandeb**, where the strait narrows to roughly 20 km. Windward assesses they now control essentially all of Yemen's Red Sea coastline.
- Reuters called it Saudi Arabia's **gravest setback in Yemen in years**, citing intelligence failure, the collapse of Yemeni allied factions, and an untested Saudi-led command structure that replaced Emirati oversight.
- A diplomat told Reuters the Houthis had quietly assembled about **100,000 fighters**: "Everyone, including the Saudis, missed this build-up." Retreating Saudi-backed forces abandoned a sizeable cache of US- and Saudi-supplied weaponry.
- Mokha fell partly to a **fabricated withdrawal order spread by radio and SMS using an AI-cloned commander's voice**, through a coalition that lacked a joint operations room.
- They are now pressing toward **Marib**, the last major government stronghold and the site of Yemen's most important oil and gas facilities. Government forces have counterattacked and held some ground northwest of Marib, so this is not yet decided.

**Why military pressure will not fix it quickly.** The Houthis have absorbed Israeli decapitation strikes that killed their prime minister and military chief of staff, and **Operation Rough Rider**, the two-month billion-dollar US bombing campaign in 2025. Neither degraded them. Saudi Arabia has flown as many as **450 airstrikes in a single week** this month; the Houthis responded by downing a Saudi **F-15** with what they say was a locally manufactured surface-to-air missile.

**And the US is staying out.** Two Western officials told Reuters that neither Washington nor European capitals were prepared to offer direct military backing, and that the US had not even joined the Saudi-led maritime defence coalition announced in July. MbS went to **Cairo** to ask Egypt to help contain the fallout after Washington declined. Pakistan and Turkey signed a joint defence pact with Riyadh last month and Pakistan's defence minister has said it is time to implement it — which is an escalation vector, not a stabilisation one.

So on the specific question "when does oil flow close to normal": there is no visible military path, no US backstop, and the one remaining lever is a negotiated deal. That is section 5.

## 2. Saudi Arabia has now lost or impaired every export route

This is the part that undercuts my own framing. I argued crude has workarounds and product does not. **The workarounds are being destroyed roughly as fast as they are being built.**

| Route | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Strait of Hormuz | Blockaded since February. Transits in single digits to mid-teens. |
| East-West pipeline (Petroline) to Yanbu | **Shut** since 10 September after a pumping-station strike. Windward: Petroline carried roughly **92% of Saudi seaborne crude exports** via Yanbu by June — about 4 million b/d, or **~4% of global supply**. |
| Yanbu out via Bab el-Mandeb | Houthi "maritime embargo" on Saudi-linked shipping since 20 July. Red Sea oil loadings fell from 3.8 to **2.2 million b/d** in August. |
| Suez / SUMED (Ain Sukhna → Sidi Kerir) then Cape | The remaining path. Roughly **70%** of Yanbu crude exports now go this way. |

The consequences, in numbers:

- **Saudi supply fell 2.3 million b/d to 6 million — the lowest in three decades** (IEA). That is the single most important figure in this memo.
- Yanbu's available stocks support only about **five to seven days** of exports with Petroline down.
- Transit to an Asian buyer like South Korea goes from **24 days to 54 days** (Kpler).
- Fourteen Saudi-flagged vessels have already rerouted via the Cape since 20 July, adding 10–14 days and $0.9–1.0 million per voyage.
- Bab el-Mandeb crossings halved to **15 a day**. Suez transits are running **40.8/day** in 2026 against 73.7/day in 2023.

**The important qualifier, against my own argument.** This is a targeted economic siege of Saudi Arabia, not a global closure. The Houthi-run coordination centre says Bab el-Mandeb stays open to everything except Saudi-linked traffic, Windward assesses with moderate confidence they will not fully close it to non-Saudi shipping, and Saudi cargoes to China and Pakistan have been allowed through. Barrels are being made longer and more expensive rather than erased. But "longer and more expensive" applied to 4% of global supply, indefinitely, is not a premium that deflates on a repair headline.

## 3. The refining bottleneck is now demonstrably a 2027 story

The duration view does not rest on Yemen alone. The physical capacity arithmetic says the same thing.

**Goldman (Yulia Zhestkova Grigsby), 29 August:**
- Global refined product exports down **6 million b/d**, with Russia and the Persian Gulf three quarters of the decline.
- Global refinery runs down about **7 million b/d** year on year; outages roughly **60% above seasonal norms**.
- 2027 diesel margins versus Brent: **$63/bbl US, $49/bbl Europe** — more than double February's $27 and $19.
- Central case: Middle East outages moderate only by **mid-2027**, while **Russian outages persist through all of 2027**. Runs do not return to normal seasonal levels until H2 2027.
- Only **400–500 kb/d of capacity additions annually** in 2026–27, "not enough to offset persistent Russian outages, let alone meet structural demand growth."
- Lower inventories account for 50–70% of the upgrade; a **$7–13/bbl security premium** is the remainder, priced at a 50% probability that outages persist.
- **If outages stay near the 2026 peak and freight stays elevated, US diesel margins hold above $80/bbl through 2027.**

**OPEC's own World Oil Outlook** makes the supply side concrete: global distillation additions are just **0.6 million b/d in 2027**, the weakest year of the 2026–2030 window, and cumulative incremental middle-distillate output potential reaches only 0.7 million b/d by 2027. Nothing arrives in time.

That is the answer to "I don't believe this gets resolved in 2026." It is not a contrarian view. It is Goldman's central case and OPEC's capacity table.

**Change to the main memo:** the stated window of "harvest now through Q1 2027 heating" is too short. Read it as **through 2027, with the 2028 delivery wave as the terminal risk** (section 4).

## 4. The tanker leg gets much better — and acquires an expiry date

If both chokepoints stay impaired, the tonne-mile expansion is enormous and structural: Saudi crude to Asia going from 24 to 54 days is not a rate cycle, it is a redrawn map. This is the cleanest multi-year expression of the view.

**But I have to add a constraint I did not have when I wrote the main memo, and it cuts against an open-ended hold.**

The orderbook has exploded:

| Segment | Orderbook / fleet, mid-2026 | Year earlier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All tankers | **19.0%** | 15.8% |
| VLCC | **31.4%** | 11.5% |
| LR1 / Panamax | 29.4% | 19.1% |
| Aframax / LR2 | **20.0%** | 17.5% |
| MR2 | 16.3% | ~16% |

- H1 2026 contracting: **407 orders versus 139** in H1 2025, up 193%.
- Vantage Shipbrokers: over **90% of 2026's orders deliver in 2028–2029** — roughly 199 vessels in 2028 and 193 in 2029, against only 20 in 2027.
- BIMCO: product tanker **supply growth of 6.5% in 2026 and 6% in 2027** against demand growth of 0–1% and 0.5–1.5%.
- Vantage flags **2028** as the first year when supply growth, the IMO Net-Zero carbon framework, and possible geopolitical de-escalation could align against owners at once.

**The counterweight, in fairness:** Xclusiv/Breakwave argue most of the 2025–2028 intake is replacement rather than net addition — MR2, LR2 and Suezmax replacement ratios of 106%, 100% and 110% against 21+ year-old tonnage, assuming zero scrapping. On that reading supply discipline holds through 2028 and the real risk is later, concentrated in VLCCs.

**One more caveat specific to my Scorpio argument.** Vantage estimates roughly **66% of the global LR2 fleet is now trading crude rather than clean products**. Published LR2 clean rates are therefore partly "a supply illusion" — they measure how much crude-trade pull exists, not clean-market health. The $64,900/day LR2 number is real revenue, but do not read it as pure diesel-market tightness.

**Net:** own the tanker leg for the 2026–2027 window that the user's view describes, and treat **2028 as the exit**, not as more of the same. The three-year Scorpio charters at $40,188 and $42,500/day look smarter in this light — the company sold forward into the delivery wave.

## 5. What actually ends this (and it isn't a battlefield)

The resolution path is a **Saudi–Houthi deal**, and the terms are already public. Per former US negotiator Dennis Ross, the Houthis want Riyadh to lift restrictions on **Hodeidah port** and **Sanaa airport**, pay **war compensation**, and **scale back support for the recognized government**. The Institute for the Study of War reads the campaign as an effort to raise Saudi political and economic costs to an untenable level and force Riyadh to halt operations.

That is the risk to the duration view, and it is capitulation rather than victory. A Gulf official described Riyadh's choices as narrowing: let the Houthis consolidate, or escalate and invite deeper strikes inside the kingdom. With no US backstop, the cheapest exit is a deal.

**So the two things that end this trade are both diplomatic, not military:**

1. A Saudi–Houthi settlement on Hodeidah and Sanaa. Reopens Bab el-Mandeb, restores Yanbu, compresses freight.
2. A Russia–Ukraine energy truce **with sanctions relief attached** — Peskov's stated price on 15 September. Restarts Kirishi, NORSI, Volgograd, Taneco.

Either one alone is survivable for the thesis. Both together end it.

## 6. Positioning under this view

**XOM — the case gets stronger. Keep as core.**
This is the instrument that pays in the user's scenario *and* in the resolution scenario. Roughly 43% of adjusted earnings from refining, chemicals and specialties captures the record crack; upstream captures elevated crude. Net debt to capital of 10.7% and a $20 billion buyback mean you are paid to wait through a long dislocation. If oil never normalises, both legs earn. Political risk — Trump naming Exxon specifically, the DOJ gouging probe, windfall-tax chatter — remains the main objection, and it gets worse the longer prices stay high.

**OXY — downgrading from sell to hold, and trimming only into strength.**
My sell call rested on a specific premise: that the crude war premium deflates because Saudi Arabia has workarounds. **Section 2 weakens that premise materially.** With Saudi supply at a three-decade low and all three export routes impaired, crude has a higher floor than I assumed, and the $260 million per $1/bbl sensitivity works for the position rather than against it.

I still prefer XOM, but the reason has changed. It is **dominance, not direction**: XOM earns in both states of the world and OXY earns in one. That argument survives the update; "crude is going down" does not. If you already sold, you have not made an error — you took a 45% gain off a single-variable position. If you did not, hold it and stop treating $55 as an entry target, because that number assumed mid-cycle crude, and this is not a mid-cycle world.

**PBF — unchanged as the high-torque refining expression, with a longer runway.**
Goldman's 2027 margin numbers and OPEC's 0.6 million b/d of 2027 additions turn this from a winter-heating trade into a two-year earnings story. The Q4 turnaround calendar (Chalmette, Paulsboro, Toledo) remains the idiosyncratic risk, and over a multi-year hold it is worth pairing PBF's torque with a quality downstream name rather than running it alone.

**Tankers — best structural expression of the view, with a 2028 exit.** See section 4.

**Add: European gas and LNG as the second multi-winter leg.**
A Bab el-Mandeb that is impaired for years also blocks the Suez route for Qatari LNG into Europe. Germany at 56% storage and TTF near $93 is not a one-winter problem in this scenario — it is the 2022 crisis with less time to refill and a longer supply chain. Cheniere is the listed beneficiary.

## 7. Markers

**Confirming the duration view:** Marib falling. Houthi retention of Perim. Petroline missing the six-week full-capacity target. Saudi loadings staying near 2.2 million b/d or below. Suez transits staying near half of pre-crisis. Distillate holding 13%+ below the five-year average through the turn of the year.

**Breaking it:** Saudi concessions on Hodeidah or Sanaa airport. A verified Russia energy truce with sanctions relief (UNGA, 21–23 September). Several consecutive EIA distillate builds toward the five-year band. WTI at or below $92.69.

**Escalation tail:** the Houthi drone toward **Mecca** — which Riyadh calls a red line — and any invocation of the Saudi–Pakistan–Turkey defence pact. That path leads somewhere much worse than a diesel squeeze, and it is not in any of these price forecasts.
