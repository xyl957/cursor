# Scenario: Trump bans diesel exports

**Date:** 17 September 2026
**Companion to:** [2026-09-17-diesel-shortage.md](2026-09-17-diesel-shortage.md)
**Short answer:** Diesel gets cheaper in Texas and more expensive almost everywhere else, including the Northeast, which is the place with the actual shortage. It is also the one lever Trump is least likely to pull, because the statute he would reach for was repealed in 2015 and his revealed preference this week is the exact opposite.

This is research, not investment advice.

---

## 1. He probably can't, not with a stroke of a pen

This is the part the market commentary is getting wrong, and it matters for how you price the risk.

**EPCA Section 103 (42 U.S.C. §6212)** was the clean authority. It let the President "by rule ... restrict exports of coal, petroleum products, natural gas, or petrochemical feedstocks." That is exactly the tool for a diesel export ban.

**It no longer exists.** Section 101(a) of division O of Pub. L. 114–113 — the December 2015 Consolidated Appropriations Act, the bill that lifted the crude export ban — **repealed §6212 outright**. The replacement, §6212a, bars federal restriction on crude exports and gives the President a narrow exception: declare a national emergency, notice it in the Federal Register, one year at a time, renewable. That exception is written for **crude oil**. It does not create a refined-products authority, because the refined-products authority was the thing being thrown away.

What survives:

- **The savings clause in §6212a(c)** preserves IEEPA, the National Emergencies Act, Trading With the Enemy Act, EPCA part B of title II (the international allocation machinery), and sanctions law. IEEPA under a declared national emergency is the realistic executive vehicle. It is also aggressive, unprecedented for this purpose, and would be in court within days — with the industry funding the plaintiffs.
- **Commerce short-supply controls, 15 C.F.R. Part 754.** Section 754.3 does cover "petroleum products not including crude oil" — but only product *derived from the Naval Petroleum Reserves*. It is not a general hook.
- **EAA 1979 §2406(e)** had a purpose-built refined-product regime (license required, five-day congressional notification, 30-day hold, a 250,000-barrel de minimis). The EAA expired in 2001 and was superseded by the Export Control Reform Act of 2018.

So the honest read: **an export ban is an act of Congress, or an IEEPA fight.** Neither is a weekend announcement. The correct market reaction to a Thune soundbite is therefore not "price the ban," it is "price a multi-month legislative and legal process with a low base rate." Newsquawk's framing is right — the tells are formal sponsorship, administration engagement, and whether it gets framed as pump prices or supply security.

## 2. What actually happens physically

Start with the arithmetic. US refiners make ~5.35 million b/d of distillate. Domestic demand is ~3.68 million b/d. The ~1.67 million b/d difference goes to the export dock and to inventory. Total product exports are running ~1.7 million b/d, matching the August record.

Ban the exports and that surplus has exactly two places to go: **into PADD 1, or nowhere.**

**It mostly goes nowhere.** McKinsey modeled this in December 2022 and the physical constraints have not changed:

- Total East Coast product imports are only ~900,000 b/d — roughly 65% of the ~1.4 million b/d the Gulf would need to rehome. Even perfect displacement of every imported barrel leaves a third of the surplus stranded.
- Gulf-to-East pipeline capacity is "largely maxed out." Colonial moves ~2.7 million b/d and Plantation ~0.7 million b/d, and both are full in tight markets.
- The rest needs Jones Act tankers, where there is little excess capacity and the economics have historically lost to Canadian imports.

So refiners do the only other thing available: **cut runs.** CIBC's Rebecca Babin put it plainly — "they would just cut their runs to meet domestic demand." AFPM's 2022 modeling put the number at ~1.3 million b/d of US refining capacity shuttered, about 7% of the national total, from trapped Gulf Coast production.

Cutting runs does not cut diesel alone. You lose the gasoline and jet that came out of the same barrel. A policy aimed at diesel becomes a gasoline problem by spring.

**Prices split three ways:**

| Market | Direction | Magnitude (McKinsey/AFPM 2022 framework) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| USGC + Midwest | **Down** | ~$10–11/bbl, roughly 20–25 cents/gal. Gulf pricing decouples from the world and falls to marginal-capacity breakeven. |
| Global / ARA gasoil | **Up hard** | Removing ~1.4 mb/d forces resupply from the least efficient capacity left: at minimum ~+$25/bbl, with real risk of fly-up pricing when no capacity clears at any price. |
| PADD 1 and PADD 5 | **Up** | Both stay import-dependent at the margin, so both keep pricing off a world market you just made more expensive. Jones Act freight also flies up, and West Coast pricing is often Gulf cost plus freight. |

AFPM's estimate: more than two-thirds of US consumers see distillate up **more than 45 cents a gallon**, with a net GDP hit above $44 billion.

## 3. The cruel irony

**The Northeast — the region with the actual crisis — is the region a ban helps least, and may hurt.**

PADD 1 runs at ~85% utilization against a demand base it cannot come close to serving. It imported ~1.1 million b/d of product in 2021, including ~250,000 b/d of distillate, much of it from Eastern Canada and Europe. New England sits at the far end of every pipeline.

And right now its most important non-US supplier is down: **Irving Oil's Saint John refinery (~320,000 b/d), Canada's largest and a key Northeast distillate source, is in a 75-day "Operation Osprey" turnaround from early September through mid-November 2026.** That is precisely the window in question.

Ban exports to Europe while Europe is scrambling into winter, and you burn the counterparty relationship that gets cargoes into New York Harbor during a January cold snap. You trade a certain, visible cent-per-gallon win in Houston for the loss of your own winter insurance in Boston. That is Burgum's California point, generalized — and it is why Interior has already said on the record that a ban would not lower prices.

One more wrinkle from AFPM worth knowing: export-grade product does not always meet US specification. Banning exports can therefore *reduce* the volume of usable domestic product rather than increase it.

## 4. What it does to the book

### PBF — gaps down on the headline, then the least bad place to be

No US refiner is a winner here. The question is only who loses least.

Every refining equity gaps down together, because the market prices a US margin regime change, not a company-by-company differentiation. The differentiation shows up in realized earnings a quarter later:

- **Valero is the most exposed.** Pure-play, Port Arthur and Corpus Christi, maximum export leverage to Latin America and Europe. It has the most to lose and it is sitting at $403 after a 140% run.
- **Marathon and Phillips 66** are cushioned by MPLX and by chemicals/midstream/marketing respectively.
- **PBF splits.** Chalmette (~175 kb/d, Gulf coker, export-oriented) gets stranded with everyone else. But Delaware City and Paulsboro sell into New York Harbor, and a ban **widens** the NYH–USGC spread rather than narrowing it: Gulf barrels get marked down to stranded-breakeven while NYH stays tethered to a world price that just went up. The spread that collapsed to 4 cents in July blows back out, because the logistics that would arbitrage it are the same maxed-out pipelines and thin Jones Act fleet as always.

That is the whole reason the primary is an East Coast refiner instead of a Gulf exporter. It was not only about where the next print is. It is also the position that survives the policy tail.

### Scorpio — I had this backwards

My earlier memo said to sell the tankers first on an export-ban headline. That is too glib, and the 2026 evidence points the other way.

**Export bans reshuffle trade toward longer voyages, and reshuffling is what product tankers get paid for.** When Russia banned diesel exports on 8 July, Europe was forced to replace short-haul Baltic barrels with long-haul cargoes from the Middle East Gulf, India, and the US Gulf. S&P Global's Fotios Katsoulas described the result as locking up "massive amounts of LR capacity." The Platts LR2 index went from **$57,204/day at the start of June to $97,872/day by 14 July.** The losers were the short-haul Primorsk-to-Rotterdam shuttles, which suffered "a permanent contraction in volume."

Apply the same logic to a US ban:

- **Kills:** USGC→UK Continent (currently the critical Atlantic fronthaul) and USGC→Brazil/Mexico/Caribbean. These are the short-haul MR trades. TC14 and TC18 collapse. A lot of Atlantic MR tonnage gets displaced and ballasts east, pressuring rates where it lands.
- **Creates:** Europe *and* all of Latin America must now source from India, Korea, and the Middle East. India-to-Europe around the Cape is roughly double the voyage of Houston-to-Rotterdam. Brazil sourcing from Asia instead of the US Gulf is a far bigger tonne-mile expansion still. That is LR2 work.

Scorpio is 25 LR2 / 36 MR / 14 Handymax. The LR2 book — already the highest-earning part of the fleet at $64,900/day — is on the right side of this. The 36 MRs are on the wrong side.

**The honest disanalogy:** Russia's banned barrels were still *produced*, they just sailed farther to different buyers. US barrels under a ban largely do not get produced at all, because refiners cut runs. So there is genuine volume destruction here that was not present in the Russia case, and it partly offsets the distance gain.

Net: **expect a violent drawdown on the headline, then a likely tonne-mile melt-up.** That is a buy-the-panic setup for LR2-weighted owners, not a stop-loss. Revised rule: an export ban impairs the MR leg and arguably enhances the LR2 leg — it is a reason to prefer Scorpio's LR2 exposure over MR-heavy Hafnia, not a reason to exit the sector.

### The actual winners

**Reliance Industries** is the single biggest beneficiary. Jamnagar is the largest export refinery complex in the world and it would inherit the Atlantic basin. European refiners (Shell, TotalEnergies, Repsol, OMV) and the Korean exporters capture the rest. If you want to own the ban, you own it offshore.

## 5. What he does instead

The realistic playbook is already running, and it is why the ban keeps not happening:

1. **Jones Act waivers.** On 17 March 2026, DHS issued the longest and broadest Jones Act waiver since at least 1950 — roughly 659 product categories, initially 60 days, extended 90 more through 16 August, with the White House expected to extend again. It has been used ~200 times. It worked: Gulf-to-East Coast movements hit a record 1.2 million b/d in April with distillate at a record 220,000 b/d, and Gulf-to-West Coast went from under 30,000 b/d to 190,000 b/d. This is the cheap, fast, legally clean lever, and unlike a ban it actually moves barrels to where the shortage is.
2. **NEHHOR.** The Northeast Home Heating Oil Reserve holds ~1 million barrels of ULSD. Release requires a presidential finding, and the statutory trigger is the Northeast residential heating oil-to-crude differential running more than 60% above its five-year rolling average for two consecutive Mondays and still rising. On this winter's setup that trigger is very likely to fire. One million barrels against PADD 1 demand is symbolic, but it is a free headline.
3. **Spec and blending relief**, jawboning refiners, and scrutiny of retail pricing.
4. **Russia sanctions relief** — which brings us to the real risk.

## 6. The tail risk is the opposite of the one you asked about

Trump's revealed preference this week is not restricting US supply. It is **restoring Russian supply.**

- 13 September: "Zelenskyy has to do one thing. He has to stop knocking out diesel fuel in Russia ... he's causing a shortage of diesel."
- 14 September: he posted that both sides had agreed to stop hitting energy targets. Neither Kyiv nor Moscow confirmed it, and Zelensky said he was "not sure" of Russian intent.
- 15 September: Peskov called it "a very good idea" — then attached the price. Safe passage for tankers, and **lift the sanctions on Russian energy**. "Only then will the world be adequately supplied with these petroleum products."
- Strikes continued anyway. Ukraine hit a Samara refinery; Russia hit Kyiv filling stations.
- A possible Trump–Zelensky meeting at UNGA, 21–23 September.

That is the trade you should actually be hedging. Roughly 25–40% of Russian refining capacity is offline from drone strikes. An energy truce that holds, plus refined-product sanctions relief, restarts Kirishi, NORSI, Volgograd, and Taneco and puts Russian diesel back into Turkey, Brazil, and North Africa — which frees US Gulf barrels that currently backfill those buyers. That unwinds the thesis from the supply side, permanently, in a way an export ban never could.

An export ban would make global diesel *tighter*. A Russia deal makes it looser. Only one of those kills the long.

Also worth holding in mind: the midterms are in November. Political pressure on diesel peaks in the next six weeks, which is exactly the window in which a bad idea gets a floor vote.

## 7. Rules

**Probability:** low, call it well under 20% for anything enacted this winter. The White House has said no twice (Burgum on the record at the G20 in Houston), the legal vehicle was repealed in 2015, and the industry opposition is total and well-funded.

**If a ban headline hits:**

- Do not sell PBF into it. The East Coast leg is the hedge; the NYH–USGC spread widens.
- Do not sell Scorpio into it either. Correction to my earlier note: the LR2 book benefits from the reshuffle. The MR leg is the impaired part.
- Do sell, or avoid adding to, Valero. Maximum export leverage at an all-time high is the worst risk-reward in the sector under this scenario.
- The clean long is offshore refining — Reliance, European refiners.

**Escalation tells to watch, in order:** a bill number and formal sponsorship; administration engagement rather than Burgum-style dismissal; framing that shifts from pump prices to supply security; and any IEEPA national-emergency notice in the Federal Register.

**De-escalation tell that matters more:** a verified Russia–Ukraine energy truce with sanctions relief attached. Watch UNGA, 21–23 September.
