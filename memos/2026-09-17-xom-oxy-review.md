# Position review: XOM and OXY

**Date:** 17 September 2026
**Companion to:** [2026-09-17-diesel-shortage.md](2026-09-17-diesel-shortage.md) · [2026-09-17-export-ban-scenario.md](2026-09-17-export-ban-scenario.md)

**Call: sell or cut OXY. Hold XOM, but don't add.**

They look like the same trade and they are not. One of them owns the bottleneck. The other owns the thing with workarounds.

This is research, not investment advice.

---

## The tape already told you

16 September, on a single Reuters headline that Saudi Arabia was offering extra crude via Oman:

| | Close | Day | YTD |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| XOM | $163.32 | **−3.54%** | ~+30–40% |
| OXY | $59.36 | **−6.55%** | ~+45% |

Same news, roughly double the damage. That is not noise, it is the structure of the two businesses. Nothing changed about either company's assets that day. What changed was the perceived durability of the war premium in crude, and only one of these two is a pure bet on it.

For reference, XLE is +48% YTD and Chevron +44%, so XOM has actually *lagged* the sector it anchors.

## OXY: you are long crude flat price, and nothing else now

**The OxyChem sale removed the hedge.** Occidental closed the $9.7 billion all-cash sale of its chemicals business to Berkshire Hathaway on 2 January 2026. Strategically it was defensible — it took principal debt to $11.8 billion, the lowest since Q2 2019, and net debt/EBITDA to ~1.36. But it also means that as of this year OXY is a **pure-play oil and gas producer**. The one segment that earned money on something other than the barrel is gone.

**The sensitivity is enormous and disclosed.** Management pegs it at roughly **$260 million of annualized cash flow per $1/bbl** change in oil. Free cash flow breakeven is around $51–55. Run that in reverse: a $20 retreat in crude is roughly $5 billion of annual cash flow, against a company with a $60 billion market cap.

**You are paying a war-premium multiple and calling it cheap.** OXY trades near 13x forward earnings, which sounds reasonable until you ask what those forward earnings assume. They assume crude in the triple digits. That is the classic commodity value trap — cheap on peak earnings. On a mid-cycle $65–70 WTI base, the normalized math puts fair entry closer to **$55**, below where it trades.

**The Street is not with you.** The rating split is 8 buys and 2 outperforms against **16 holds**, 1 underperform and 1 sell. Mean target $67 on 25 analysts, about 9% upside. DBS carries a HOLD with a **$58 target — below the current price**. TIKR's mid-case values OXY at $65 by 2030, roughly 2% annualized. The dividend is 1.6%, so you are not being paid to wait.

**Two company-specific items worth naming:** capex for 2027 has been discussed starting around $5.9 billion, which eats into the free cash flow story; and Richard Jackson took over as CEO from Vicki Hollub on 1 June 2026 and has not been tested through a downcycle.

### The decisive argument

Read it against the diesel thesis. The whole point of that memo is that **crude has workarounds and refined product does not.** The US is producing 13.9 million b/d. Saudi Arabia is doing ship-to-ship transfers outside Hormuz. Venezuela is being reopened to US operators. Brent has already round-tripped from $130 to $107 on individual supply headlines.

Now look at what kills the thesis: a Russia–Ukraine energy truce with sanctions relief attached, which Peskov explicitly priced on 15 September. In that scenario crude falls hard — and OXY has no offset, because it does not own a refinery and cannot capture the crack that would stay wide.

**OXY gives you the risk of this trade without the payoff of it.** It is correlated to the bad outcome and only partially participates in the good one. That is the wrong side of the asymmetry.

## XOM: the mega-cap that actually owns the bottleneck

Exxon is the largest refiner outside China, and it is being paid for it.

**Q2 2026 adjusted earnings by segment ($m):**

| Segment | Q2 2026 | Q1 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upstream | 9,189 | 6,265 |
| **Energy Products (refining)** | **4,099** | 2,799 |
| Chemical Products | 1,214 | 110 |
| Specialty Products | 969 | 651 |
| Corporate & financing | (791) | (1,053) |
| **Total** | **14,680** | 8,772 |

Energy Products, Chemical and Specialty together are **~43% of adjusted earnings**. On a GAAP basis Energy Products swung from −$1,262m in Q1 to +$5,465m in Q2. The company called out **record second-quarter diesel production**. That is the diesel crack showing up directly in a mega-cap income statement.

**The natural hedge is the point.** Stillwater's work on the current dislocation found diesel's correlation to crude has collapsed — R² from ~0.70 to ~0.40, with a $1 move in crude now passing only about 5 cents to diesel. The asymmetry that creates is refiner-friendly and producer-hostile: **crude down widens the crack; crude up squeezes it.** An integrated with 43% downstream earnings is built for exactly the scenario that would gut a pure E&P.

Note also that Hormuz has been a *negative* for Exxon's upstream — Middle East disruptions cut Upstream earnings by $1.1 billion in Q2 and $1.3 billion year to date. Much of what you own here is not war premium.

**Balance sheet and returns are not the issue.** Net debt to capital 10.7%. Net debt cut $7 billion in Q2. First-half operating cash flow $32.3 billion, free cash flow $17.2 billion in Q2 alone, $8.6 billion of dividends and $10.0 billion of buybacks in 1H against a $20 billion 2026 repurchase plan. Guyana's fifth FPSO sailed with 4Q26 startup adding 250 kb/d — volume growth that does not depend on price.

### Why hold rather than add

1. **Political risk is live and specifically aimed.** On 3 August Trump said Exxon and Chevron are "making too much money" and should "give some of that back to the public." He has ordered DOJ to examine price gouging, the FTC is monitoring, and there are active calls for a windfall profits tax. This risk is nasty precisely because it only bites when the thesis is working.
2. **Valuation is full.** Roughly 13.7x forward, the richest in its peer group, with Street targets clustered $177–$185 — about 9–13% upside. On peer comparisons its levered free cash flow margin (~5.7%) and return on total capital (~8.2%) rank last among the majors.
3. **It is still a macro instrument.** As one write-up put it, a commodity-price story wrapped around a well-run operator. Size accordingly.

## What to do

**Sell OXY, or cut it hard.** You have a ~45% year-to-date gain in a name that is now a single-variable bet on a war premium the President is actively working to deflate, with the sell-side clustered on hold and at least one target below spot. If you want to keep a toe in, the disciplined re-entry is nearer **$55**, where the mid-cycle math stops requiring crude to cooperate. There is likely a meaningful taxable gain to manage — worth checking the lot-level basis before you size the trade.

**Hold XOM.** It is the cheapest way to stay long the diesel crack inside a mega-cap balance sheet, and it is the position that survives a Russia deal. But it is not the next buy: the multiple is full, the sector has outrun it, and it wears the political target.

**Where the proceeds go**, if you want the capital working the actual thesis rather than the adjacent one: PBF for East Coast heating oil, and the LR2-weighted tanker leg. Both are laid out in the [main memo](2026-09-17-diesel-shortage.md). Cash is also a legitimate answer here — you are taking money off a crowded crude trade, and you are not obliged to redeploy it into an energy tape that is up 48% year to date.

**What would change the OXY call:** a genuine Hormuz closure extending into 2027 with no Saudi or Venezuelan workaround, which would put crude structurally above $100 and make the $260m-per-dollar sensitivity work for you instead of against you. Watch the same tells as the main memo — Hormuz transit counts, and UNGA on 21–23 September.
